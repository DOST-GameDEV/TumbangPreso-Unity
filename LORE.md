# TUMP: the game that grew from the street

**Visual expression:** this lore belongs to TUMP's existing cute blocky world.
New creatures, skill models and places must look native to that game, with
purposeful restrained detail. Cultural or dramatic ambition is not permission to
switch to realistic-looking fantasy anatomy. Follow the standing
[model-style rule](docs/Art_Direction.md#0--new-models-must-belong-to-tump).
Character identity should also appear in distinct casting and skill-VFX motion;
repeated generic animation is not a substitute (the same document, section0.1).

Creative foundation, 2026-09-09. This connects the existing game, rather than
adding a campaign or asking the player to learn a fictional history.

## The premise

In this world, tumbang preso grew into one of the world's favorite spectator
sports. The Philippines remains its cultural home and one of its strongest
competitors. Major tournaments draw crowds and make household names, but most
players still learn the same way: someone finds a can, someone marks the ground,
and someone says they can knock it down from farther away.

The game never outgrew that invitation. A good court can be a neighborhood street,
a public plaza or a cleared space beneath the train line. Famous players return
to these places because the important part fits anywhere: four people watching
one lata, each deciding when to risk the run for their tsinelas.

TUMP is about being there when an ordinary match becomes the story everybody
tells afterward. The impossible bank. The brave retrieval. The taya who almost
had it. The person who lost by one point and immediately asked for another game.

There is no war to win, hidden villain or world-ending threat. Winning matters
because you know the people watching. Losing matters because you will see them
again tomorrow.

## How the world and the rules fit

The professional sport kept the rotating taya. Every player must defend as well
as attack; there is no permanent hero's role and no teammate to blame for every
mistake. Rankings and tournament heats recognize individual players, while local
clubs and neighborhoods give those players a place to belong.

The international scene is larger than the matches shown here. It explains the
ranked ladder, player profiles and competitive ambition. It does not require a
new national-team system, fictional currency, stadium mode or championship menu.
The game we play is still the small court, not a simulation of an entire league.

### Classic

Classic is the widely played street discipline and a respected competitive sport
in its own right. Precision, nerve, body positioning and familiarity with your
tsinelas are enough. Older neighbors can recognize a clever play immediately.
The simple equipment is a source of pride, not evidence of an unfinished game.

### Hero Strike

Hero Strike is a separate sporting discipline in the same world. Its athletes
bring extraordinary, highly practiced signature abilities to the court. Fire,
ice, lightning and stranger talents change an opening, a route or a moment of
pressure; the can and the retrieval still decide what matters.

These abilities belong to sporting contests. Their visual language is expressive
and exaggerated, with brief, contained effects and no lasting injury or destroyed
neighborhood. A dramatic impact is a sporting setback. A punch or lunge is read
as an attempt to tag or interrupt, never an attempt to kill. There are no guns.
The world accepts these talents without a compulsory science lesson or a new
magic-resource system for the player to memorize.

Classic players need not aspire to Hero Strike, and Hero Strike does not make
Classic obsolete. Some spectators love the clean street contest; others follow
particular athletes and the way they transform it. Both have prestige and their
own pleasures.

## The places

Keep the Philippines recognizable. Improve the specificity of materials, signs,
planting, shade, architecture and everyday use before adding a fictional symbol.
The maps are places with a court in them, not corridors decorated with flags.

| Map | Place in the world | What the environment should communicate |
|---|---|---|
| Eskinita | The neighborhood court where everyone already knows who likes to throw from which side | A sari-sari frontage, homes, laundry, plants, worn street surfaces and roadside seating. Close human scale, a clear lane and warm domestic life |
| Bayan Plaza | A public square used for local matches and community gatherings | Civic space, shade trees, a monument, familiar church/basketball vocabulary and places for a crowd to stand. More open and composed than Eskinita |
| Ilalim ng Tulay | A marked urban court under the rail guideway, drawing on the team's Gilmore/LRT reference | The weight of the viaduct, electronics shops, pisonet, pares and the jeepney. Selective shop light, real urban depth and the occasional train overhead |

Eskinita and Bayan Plaza are composites of recognizable Philippine places, not
claims to be exact surveyed streets. Ilalim uses its existing real-place reference;
new details should be checked against that place before adding a landmark or sign.
Do not attach invented historical claims to real monuments or businesses.

In the sporting world these are designated playing spaces, cleared for a match.
Traffic does not run through active play. Street furniture belongs where people
would use it: chairs by a frontage, a food counter by its customers, planters at
an edge. A bench standing arbitrarily in the chase line needs a reason or a move.
Empty playable ground can be deliberate; empty surroundings are a composition
problem, not an instruction to scatter props everywhere.

## The people

The Classic cast carries the social range of a neighborhood. Familiar names and
forms of address already do useful work: kuya, ate, lola, mang and aling suggest
relationships without a wall of exposition. Keep their distinct faces, skin,
clothes and temperaments. Do not turn every person into the same teenage athlete
or make older characters a joke about being old.

The six Hero Strike athletes are recognizable personalities before they use a
power. These are fictional characterization directions for the existing cast,
not biographies of the real people their names may reference.

| Hero | Character thread | How it should show up in play and presentation |
|---|---|---|
| Sean | Waits for the line he wants, then commits completely. He would rather be remembered for one clean, brave play than constant activity | A gathered stance, a decisive extension and a controlled recovery. Fire follows his commitment; it does not obscure what he chose |
| Zack | Notices an angle before the rest of the court does. Enjoys making a difficult play look almost casual | Precise footwork, a small pause before the snap, narrow branching electricity. Confidence comes from accuracy rather than swagger in every idle |
| Dante | The player people trust to hold the difficult space. Patient, grounded and hard to rush | A planted base, protective shapes, broken stone with weight. His strongest motion begins in the floor rather than with a generic leap |
| Cheska | Reads a crowded court as a set of useful spaces. Practical, observant and quietly competitive | Exact gestures, clear ice boundaries and a composed finish. She changes the route with the least motion needed |
| Nemu | Often looks as if she is paying attention to something else, until the opening she anticipated appears. Kuro belongs at her side | Light, slightly uncanny motion, inward effects and patient timing. The ghostly identity is curious and playful, not a threat to the neighborhood |
| Phaister | Enjoys the audience almost as much as the contest. Treats a good setup like a performance whose reveal should arrive at exactly the right time | Deliberate flourishes, readable sigils and an open finish. Stagecraft and sporting mischief, not cruelty or a sinister conquest |

Their rivalry is affectionate without making every character friendly in the same
way. Dante may respect Cheska's discipline, Zack may enjoy testing Sean's patience,
and Phaister may try to get a reaction out of Nemu. Those small contrasts can inform
a pose or short description. They do not need dialogue trees, relationship meters
or new cutscenes.

Filipino character should be a natural hint where it suits a design: practical
sportswear, a familiar towel or bag strap, a locally recognizable material, a way
of resting between plays. Avoid compulsory traditional clothing or the same flag
patch on every hero. Preserve the existing identities and let the place connect
them as much as costume does.

## A shared artistic language

Warm printed color, tactile material, expressive voxel silhouettes and small
imperfections connect the game. The UI borrows the confidence of a hand-lettered
local match poster, with the calm spacing of something somebody cared to arrange.
The world carries worn concrete, tin, rubber, cloth and leafy shade. Heroes bring
their own accents into that world without repainting it in six competing palettes.

Sound follows the same hierarchy. Rubber on the street, a tin crack, approaching
feet and a short scrape should communicate the contest before a voice explains
it. Powers have distinct attacks and tails, with room between them. Neighborly
life sits at the edges. Not every Filipino moment needs a shouted Tagalog phrase.

The main-menu loop is the breath before the match: an ordinary street, the can,
the tsinelas and people who belong there. It promises play rather than showing
the loudest possible ultimate on repeat.

## What reaches the player

Use this foundation to choose environmental details, character descriptions,
menu copy, gestures, sound and presentation priorities. Most stays in this file.
One short personality line in character selection is more useful than a required
biography. Ability text must still explain the ability, and a lobby must still
explain who is ready. Lore never displaces information needed to play.

A useful tone reference is simply: **It began on an ordinary street.**
Keep that warmth even when the competition becomes serious. Player-facing copy
stays in English; identity comes through place, character, material and play.
